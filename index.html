Foundations

Understanding giftedness & underachievement

The concepts every section of this toolkit builds on.

Definition

What does "gifted" mean?

A student who demonstrates, or has the potential to demonstrate, ability significantly beyond same-age peers in one or more domains — intellectual, creative, social-emotional, or physical/sensorimotor. Giftedness is a potential, not a guarantee of high performance: whether that potential turns into visible achievement depends heavily on the conditions around the student (see Gagné's model, below).

Definition

What is an "underachieving gifted" student?

A student with demonstrated high ability or potential whose actual output — grades, work completed, engagement — sits persistently and significantly below that potential. It isn't laziness by default: it is usually a signal that something in the student, the environment, or both, is blocking the path from ability to achievement.

Concept

What is a "ceiling"?

Many gifted students coast through early schooling on natural ability alone, without ever needing to develop study strategies, planning, or tolerance for struggle. A ceiling is the point — often at a stage transition like Year 8 or Year 10 — where content finally demands more than raw ability. The student hits real difficulty for the first time and doesn't know how to work through it, because they've never had to learn how. This can look like sudden disengagement, avoidance, or a sharp grade drop.

Concept

Student voice & choice

Giving students real input into what they learn, how they show it, and at what pace is one of the most consistent protective factors against underachievement. Choice restores a sense of ownership and relevance that many gifted students lose when work feels pre-decided and too easy.

Anchor framework: Gagné's DMGT

The Differentiating Model of Giftedness and Talent (DMGT), developed by Françoys Gagné, is the framework this toolkit uses to explain giftedness and underachievement (see gagnefrancoys.wixsite.com/dmgt-mddt).

Gagné separates gifts — natural, untrained abilities in intellectual, creative, social-emotional, or sensorimotor domains — from talents — well-developed skills in a specific field, such as mathematics, music, or athletics. Gifts only become talents through a developmental process of learning, training and deliberate practice.

That process is shaped by catalysts:

Intrapersonal catalysts

Traits, physical/mental characteristics, motivation, volition, self-management and self-awareness — what the student brings to the process.

Environmental catalysts

Milieu, the people around the student, the provisions available to them, and significant events — what the world does to support or block that process.

Underachievement, in DMGT terms, happens when these catalysts block or fail to support the gift-to-talent pathway — not because the gift itself is missing.

Anchor framework for enrichment: the Williams Model

Frank Williams' model is used throughout the Enrichment Task Bank as a lens for designing tasks that build both thinking and feeling processes alongside subject content — not as a rigid checklist, but as a source of variety so every task doesn't ask for the same kind of thinking.

Fluency Flexibility Originality Elaboration Risk-Taking Complexity Curiosity Imagination

Every task in the enrichment bank is tagged with whichever of these processes it naturally draws on — a task might hit two or three at once — so you can filter for the kind of thinking you want a lesson to stretch.

Why Students Underachieve
Anchored on Gagné's DMGT

Why does a gifted student underachieve?

Underachievement isn't one thing — it's what happens when the pathway from ability to achievement gets blocked. Gagné's DMGT gives us two places to look: inside the student, and around them.

Intrapersonal factors

Poor self-regulation and planning skills that were never needed until now; low self-efficacy despite high ability ("I'm not actually smart, I've just been lucky"); perfectionism that makes starting feel too risky; low motivation when work feels irrelevant or already mastered; anxiety and fear of failure.

Environmental factors

Curriculum pitched below the student's actual level; a classroom culture that rewards speed and compliance over depth; family or peer dynamics that discourage visible effort; under-identification, so the student's needs are never actually recognised; a lack of role models or provisions that stretch them.

What the research says

Research on this question spans systematic reviews mapping the internal and external factors behind gifted underachievement, meta-analyses on which interventions actually reverse it once it's set in, and ongoing work reconceptualising how underachievement itself should be defined — a reminder that this is an active, evolving area of research, not a solved problem.

Sources:

  • Unpacking the underachievement of gifted students: a systematic review of internal and external factors (2024)
    Read summary

    This systematic review by Raoof and colleagues (Heliyon, 2024) analysed 33 peer-reviewed studies from 2010 to 2024, drawn from Google Scholar, ERIC and ScienceDirect, to map what actually drives underachievement in gifted students. It sorts causes into two clusters. Internal factors include motivational issues such as low self-efficacy and poor goal valuation, emotional-social challenges like perfectionism, anxiety and low resilience, and demographic variables such as gender, age and grade level. External factors include family dynamics and parental involvement, peer relationships and social acceptance, classroom environment and teacher attitudes, and broader socioeconomic and cultural conditions. The review’s central conclusion is that no single lever fixes underachievement: internal and external factors interact and compound each other, so effective responses need to be holistic, addressing psychological factors and the surrounding environment together, rather than treating underachievement as purely an academic or purely an emotional problem that can be solved in isolation from the rest of a student’s life.

  • The identification of gifted underachievement: Validity evidence for the commonly used methods (2022)
    Read summary

    Jackson and Jung (British Journal of Educational Psychology, 2022) tackle a foundational question head-on: is gifted underachievement a real, identifiable phenomenon, or just an inconsistent label applied by whoever happens to be looking? Grounding their work in the field’s standard definition — a severe discrepancy between a student’s expected and actual achievement, not attributable to disability — they draw on prevalence research showing the pattern is genuinely common: 10–20% of gifted students show significant underachievement, and some studies put it above 50% depending on how it’s measured. Using archival data from over 2,599 students across multiple assessment instruments (OLSAT, HSC, School Certificate, NAPLAN and school assessments), they compared five commonly used identification methods against each other using convergence measures and latent class analysis. The key finding is that these methods do not agree with each other — the simple difference method (comparing standardised expected versus actual achievement) showed the strongest validity, while teacher nomination performed worst. For teachers, the practical message is twofold: underachievement is a genuine, measurable pattern with real socio-emotional correlates like self-concept and anxiety, not just a subjective label — but which students get identified as underachieving depends heavily on which method is used to look for them.

  • Gifted Underachievement: The Causes of Gifted Underachievement, and Interventions to Reverse this Pattern (2016)
    Read summary

    Wu (ANU Undergraduate Research Journal, 2016) opens with a striking Victorian government inquiry finding: up to 50% of gifted students do not perform at the level they are actually capable of. The paper draws a clear distinction between simply possessing a gift and actually developing it into achievement, arguing that turning one into the other depends on specific intrapersonal and environmental conditions being in place — it doesn’t happen automatically just because a student is capable. Combining interviews with a review of the existing literature, Wu identifies motivation, peer influence and self-esteem as the critical catalysts that determine whether a gifted student’s potential actually converts into results. The paper goes on to evaluate the school-based interventions already in use against these factors, and recommends practical strategies for shaping a learning environment that nurtures motivation, healthy peer dynamics and self-esteem together, rather than addressing academic performance in isolation from the social and emotional conditions that make it possible.

Behaviour
Anchored on James T. Webb

When underachievement looks like misbehaviour

Boredom, frustration and a mismatch between ability and task can look a lot like defiance, inattention, or disengagement — and gifted students are frequently misread, or even misdiagnosed, as a result.

Boredom-driven disruption

Under-challenged students may act out, daydream, or disengage — not because they can't do the work, but because there's nothing in it that requires their attention.

Misdiagnosis risk

James T. Webb's work highlights how traits common in gifted students — intensity, argumentativeness, inattention to uninteresting tasks — can be mistaken for ADHD, ODD, or other conditions, sometimes obscuring the real issue: unmet need.

Defiance as a control response

When a student has no meaningful say in their work, refusal can become one of the only forms of control available to them — connecting straight back to student voice and choice.

The passion connection

Behaviour that looks like defiance or disengagement often eases once a student has a genuine passion area they're allowed to go deep on. It isn't a reward dangled for good behaviour — for many of these students it's the missing piece that reconnects effort with purpose, and the behaviour settles as a side effect of that, not a precondition for it.

Not sure what a student's passion area is? The SPARK Passion Survey is a quick tool that helps students identify their passion area.

What the research says

James T. Webb's Misdiagnosis and Dual Diagnoses of Gifted Children and Adults remains the foundational text on this issue. More recent research has continued to explore the overlap and distinction between giftedness and conditions like ADHD.

  • Misdiagnosis and Dual Diagnosis of Gifted Children — James T. Webb
    Read summary

    James T. Webb’s foundational text argues that gifted children and adults are frequently misdiagnosed because clinicians mistake traits that are simply part of giftedness for symptoms of a disorder. Intensity, heightened sensory awareness and restlessness — what one parent quoted in the book calls “anything worth doing is worth doing to excess” — combined with an intense drive to question and understand, can look like ADHD, especially when paired with impatience and high motor activity, even though these students typically show sharp, sustained focus once genuinely engaged. Idealism and a habit of questioning authority can be read as Oppositional Defiant Disorder, particularly when a student is criticised for the very traits that make them gifted in the first place. Webb also documents gifted children misdiagnosed with Asperger’s, Bipolar Disorder and OCD based on traits like aloofness, emotional intensity and perfectionism. Crucially, he also covers genuine dual diagnoses — gifted children who do have coexisting learning disabilities, sleep disorders or other conditions that giftedness can mask rather than cause.

  • Cognitive characteristics of intellectually gifted children with a diagnosis of ADHD (2023) — free preprint
    Read summary

    Cornoldi, Giofrè and Toffalini (Intelligence, 2023) studied 82 twice-exceptional children who carried both high intellectual ability and a formal ADHD diagnosis — representing 8.8% of the ADHD sample studied, roughly double the rate of giftedness found in the general population. These children scored very highly on the General Ability Index of the WISC-IV (125 or above), but despite that strong overall ability, they showed consistently lower scores on working memory and processing speed — a cognitive profile that resembled gifted children with Specific Learning Disorders more closely than it resembled typically developing gifted children. Importantly, the study’s evidence suggested these ADHD diagnoses were generally valid rather than a case of giftedness being mistaken for ADHD: the children displayed the executive function and processing patterns associated with genuine ADHD, layered on top of exceptional reasoning ability. The takeaway for teachers is that giftedness and ADHD can and do co-occur at meaningfully higher rates than chance, producing a distinctive profile that combines real strength with real, separate challenge.

  • Reframing Gifted Education: From Sorting Students to Cultivating Classroom-Based Opportunities — Del Siegle & Talbot S. Hook (2026)
    Read summary

    Argues that gifted education is hampered by identification systems that remain inconsistent, inequitable, and poorly connected to actual services, and that the field should shift from asking “who is gifted?” to “how do we create conditions that let talent develop?” Proposes a Three Legs of Talent Development model — advanced content access, intellectually stimulating instruction (depth, complexity, inquiry, risk-taking), and interest-based authentic learning — delivered mainly through the general classroom rather than segregated pull-out programs, making opportunity-rich teaching the primary way to grow talent in all students. The field is currently split between the Gifted Child Paradigm and the Talent Development Paradigm, which the authors argue prevents coherent policy and practice; identification systems produce dramatically different groups of students depending on the measures, norms, cutoffs and decision rules used, and persistent inequities across demographic groups are structural rather than something better testing alone can fix.

Student Passion Area

Passion is not a bonus activity bolted onto the "real" curriculum — it's one of the levers that actually reconnects underachieving gifted students with effort. Siegle and Hook's Three Legs of Talent Development model (cited above) gives a practical structure for building it into everyday teaching, and Reis, Renzulli & Renzulli's work on enrichment pedagogy backs each leg with decades of classroom-tested practice, rather than treating passion as a separate program.

Access to advanced content

Compacting the parts of the curriculum a student has already mastered — and using flexible acceleration where appropriate — frees up real classroom time that can be redirected toward their passion area, instead of asking them to simply do more of the same work at the same pace as everyone else. Reis, Renzulli & Renzulli note that research supports eliminating as much as 50–75% of the regular curriculum for gifted students who have already mastered it, substituting more challenging material in its place — a concrete sense of how much room compacting can actually open up.

Intellectually stimulating instruction

Depth, complexity, inquiry and genuine risk-taking keep a passion area intellectually alive rather than letting it become a shallow reward activity. Encourage open-ended questions, real problems without a single correct answer, and room to go further than the task technically asks for. Kaplan's depth-and-complexity framework, used within Reis, Renzulli & Renzulli's Enrichment Triad Model, gives this a concrete shape: have students dig into a topic's details, patterns, trends, unanswered questions, ethics, and big ideas, rather than stopping at surface-level facts.

Interest-based authentic learning

This is where passion identification does the most work. Once a student's passion area is known — the SPARK Passion Survey is a quick way to surface it — connect it to real disciplinary work: an authentic audience, a genuine problem in that field, or a product that matters beyond the classroom, rather than a generic "choose your own topic" worksheet. Reis, Renzulli & Renzulli call this Type III Enrichment — the student takes on the role of a first-hand inquirer rather than a consumer of pre-packaged content — and their longitudinal research found that students who completed this kind of project maintained those interests and carried matching career aspirations all the way through college and graduate school.

Social-Emotional
Anchored on Dabrowski

The inner experience of being gifted

Many gifted students feel things — and think about things — more intensely than their peers. Kazimierz Dabrowski's theory gives language to that intensity.

Overexcitabilities

Dabrowski described five heightened modes of response: psychomotor, sensual, intellectual, imaginational, and emotional. A student with strong emotional or intellectual overexcitability may feel everyday events far more intensely than peers — which can look like "overreacting" but is a genuine difference in intensity.

Asynchronous development

A student can be years ahead cognitively while being entirely age-typical — or even behind — socially and emotionally. That gap is often invisible to adults who only see the academic side, and it can be a major source of friction and isolation.

Perfectionism

When a student has rarely had to try, failure feels catastrophic rather than normal. Avoidance of starting a task can be safer than risking an imperfect result.

What the research says

Dabrowski's Theory of Positive Disintegration remains the central framework for understanding gifted emotional intensity, and continues to be studied and applied in gifted education research.

  • Overexcitabilities in Young Highly to Profoundly Gifted Learners — open access
    Read summary

    Wood, Bouchard, De Wit, Martinson and Van Petegem (Education Sciences, 2024) ran a mixed-methods study of 88 highly to profoundly gifted children aged 4–13 (WISC scores of 140 or above) from the United States and Belgium, using parent questionnaires, developmental histories and semi-structured interviews, framed through Dabrowski’s theory of overexcitability. Nearly every participant — 99% — showed three or more overexcitabilities operating together, with emotional, intellectual and imaginational overexcitability the most common combination; intellectual (86%) and emotional (82%) overexcitability were close to universal in this group. Interview data also surfaced what Dabrowski called “developmental dynamisms” — higher-order traits including a drive toward autonomy, perfectionism, and deep empathy. Boys and girls showed comparable profiles, and 89% of families reported a history of giftedness across generations. The authors argue overexcitability assessment should be built into gifted identification itself, and call for universal screening at kindergarten entry alongside professional training to prevent these traits being misread as behavioural or emotional disorders.

  • Wood et al., overexcitabilities & gifted learners (2024)
    Read summary

    This companion piece from the Gifted Consortium summarises the same underlying research by Wood and colleagues (2024) in a more practitioner-facing form. It restates the core finding — that among highly and profoundly gifted children aged 4 to 13, almost all (99%) show three or more of Dabrowski’s five overexcitabilities operating together, most often the emotional, intellectual and imaginational combination — and translates it into practical implications for schools rather than academic framing. Where the original journal article speaks to researchers, this version is aimed at the educators, parents and clinicians who actually encounter these children day to day, and it repeats the study’s central recommendation: overexcitabilities should be actively assessed as part of gifted identification, not treated as an unrelated behavioural footnote. It’s included here alongside the primary journal article because it makes the same evidence base easier to hand directly to colleagues who want the practical version rather than the full methods section.

  • Tasmanian Association for the Gifted — explaining overexcitabilities
    Read summary

    This explainer from the Tasmanian Association for the Gifted lays out Kazimierz Dabrowski’s five overexcitabilities in plain, classroom-friendly language, without the density of the original academic sources. Psychomotor overexcitability shows up as excess physical energy and restlessness — a student who finds it genuinely hard to stay still. Sensual overexcitability is a heightened reaction to sensory input: smells, tastes, textures, sounds that other students barely register. Intellectual overexcitability is a strong, sometimes relentless drive toward curiosity and the pursuit of knowledge. Imaginational overexcitability produces a rich inner fantasy life and strong creative tendencies. Emotional overexcitability brings intense feelings, a wide emotional range and deep empathy for others. The page’s key message for teachers is balance: these traits can be a genuine source of creativity and compassion, but the same intensity can tip into overwhelm and anxiety without support, so recognising overexcitability early is what allows adults to respond to it constructively rather than as simple misbehaviour.

Multi-Exceptional
Anchored on Susan Baum

When giftedness and a learning difficulty co-occur

A student can be gifted and have a diagnosed learning difficulty, such as dyslexia, ADHD, or autism, at the same time. These students — once called "twice-exceptional," now more often "multi-exceptional" to reflect that more than one exceptionality can be involved — are some of the most frequently missed students in a school, in both directions.

The masking effect

Giftedness can compensate for a learning difficulty well enough that it goes unnoticed for years. A learning difficulty can suppress test scores and output enough that the giftedness is never identified. Or the two can cancel each other out, leaving a student who tests as "perfectly average" while being neither — and receiving no support for either.

Under-identification

Multi-exceptional students are consistently under-identified for gifted programs, because most identification processes look for consistently high performance across the board — something these students, by definition, often can't show.

Strengths-based support

Susan Baum's work emphasises identifying and building on the student's areas of strength first, rather than starting from the deficit — using their giftedness as the entry point into supporting the learning difficulty, not treating the two as separate problems to solve one at a time.

Anchor framework: Baum's Talent Centered Model

Susan Baum's Talent Centered Model is a strengths-first framework for supporting multi-exceptional students, deliberately flexible so it can be adapted to each student's particular profile rather than applied as a one-size-fits-all program. It works through six elements held together at once, not tackled one at a time:

1. Talent development

Actively nurturing the student's genuine strengths and abilities, not just managing the difficulty around them.

2. Appropriate learning environment

A supportive space built for a learner who is both advanced and struggling at once.

3. Intellectually challenging curriculum

Real rigour — underachievement risk rises further if the gifted side is left unchallenged while the difficulty is being addressed.

4. Differentiated instruction & accommodations

Teaching and adjustments tailored to the individual student's specific combination of strengths and needs.

5. Targeted remediation

Direct, specific support for the actual skill gaps involved — not a generic catch-all intervention.

6. Social & emotional support

Attending to the wellbeing cost of navigating both exceptionalities at once, which is significant and easy to overlook.

Notably, Baum's model is designed to work in a regular classroom with support from counsellors and gifted resource teachers — it doesn't require a specialised program to get started.

What the research says

Alongside Baum's own foundational work — including her widely-used operational definition of twice-exceptional learners, developed with Sally Reis and Edith Burke, and her earlier "dual differentiation" approach to curriculum for gifted students with learning disabilities — Australian research specifically on this population is still developing. Existing prevalence estimates and advocacy work point the same direction as Baum's work internationally: these students exist in meaningful numbers in Australian classrooms and are routinely missed by identification processes designed around a single profile of giftedness.

  • Dual Differentiation: An Approach for Meeting the Curricular Needs of Gifted Students with Learning Disabilities — Baum (free PDF)
    Read summary

    Baum, Cooper and Neu (Psychology in the Schools, 2001) introduce “dual differentiation” as an answer to a specific classroom dilemma: academically talented students who also struggle with reading, mathematics, spelling, handwriting or organisational skills, and who become frustrated early because schools default to remediation alone while their underlying talent stays invisible and undeveloped. Drawing on the federally funded Project HIGH HOPES (1993–1996), the authors argue schools shouldn’t have to choose between talent development and remediation — dual differentiation does both at once, developing a student’s genuine strengths while explicitly teaching strategies to compensate for specific weaknesses. Practically, this means maintaining sophisticated, advanced-level content and genuine engagement with authentic, real-world problems, rather than lowering the ceiling because a student also needs support. The paper’s core contribution is naming the masking effect directly — disability hiding giftedness, and giftedness hiding disability — and insisting both exceptionalities need to be recognised and nurtured together, not addressed one after the other.

  • Twice-exceptional students: a systematic review to outline the distinctive characteristics through a multidimensional lens (2025)
    Read summary

    Rizzo, Pinnelli and Minnaert (Frontiers in Education, 2025) systematically reviewed 19 empirical studies published between 2013 and 2025, drawn from Web of Science, SCOPUS, EBSCO Education Source and ERIC, to map what actually distinguishes twice-exceptional students from gifted peers, students with a diagnosed difficulty alone, and typically developing students. They organise the findings across six domains. Cognitively, 2e students show pronounced intra-individual variability — strong abstract reasoning sitting alongside real weaknesses in processing speed and working memory. Academically, advanced reasoning often doesn’t translate into written output, and rapid initial learning can struggle to consolidate into a stable skill. Socially, peer difficulties, isolation and feeling misunderstood are common, even alongside genuinely strong communication ability. Emotionally, heightened sensitivity acts as both a resource and a vulnerability, and physically, fine-motor weaknesses and fatigue from constant compensatory effort show up repeatedly. The review’s central conclusion, directly useful for identification: no single diagnostic profile of twice-exceptionality exists, so relying on IQ scores alone will miss students whose profile doesn’t fit the pattern — personalised, multidimensional assessment is what the evidence actually supports.

  • Special Education Status and Underidentification of Twice-Exceptional Students: Insights from ECLS-K Data (2024)
    Read summary

    Jolly and Barnard-Brak (Education Sciences, 2024) used the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study—Kindergarten, a nationally representative US sample of roughly 18,174 students, to put a hard number on under-identification. Focusing on 1,582 students who had an Individualized Education Plan by third grade, they used logistic regression to match reading, maths and executive-functioning scores against the students who had already been identified as gifted without a disability. The result: 17–18% more students with disabilities should have been identified for gifted programs based on achievement scores alone — roughly 1 in 9 students currently in gifted programs should statistically be twice-exceptional but aren’t recognised as such. The students most likely to be missed were male, non-White (Hispanic, African American and Asian students), from low-income households, or showing internalising problems like anxiety and depression, attention difficulties, or poor inhibitory control. The clear message for identification processes: disability status itself is acting as a barrier to being seen as gifted, independent of a student’s actual achievement.

  • Twice-Exceptionality in Australia: Prevalence Estimates — Griffith University coverage of the study (2020, free)
    Read summary

    Dr Michelle Ronksley-Pavia’s study (Australasian Journal for the Education of the Gifted, 2020), conducted through Griffith University, tackles a question with real funding and policy consequences: how many twice-exceptional students are actually in Australian schools? Her prevalence estimates range from a conservative 2% — roughly 78,668 students — to a more progressive 7%, or around 283,204 students, depending on which identification criteria are applied. The headline argument is that these students are significantly under-recognised in practice, well below even the conservative estimate, because current identification systems aren’t built to catch the combination. Ronksley-Pavia directly challenges the common assumption that giftedness and disability sit at opposite ends of a single spectrum, stating plainly that there is no correlation between the two — a student can be highly capable and have a genuine learning difficulty at the same time, with neither cancelling the other out. She calls for dedicated policy development and targeted funding so these students stop falling through the gap between two separate systems.

  • Compounded Disadvantage: Issues in Addressing the Educational Requirements of Twice-Exceptional Students in Schools (2025)
    Read summary

    Ronksley-Pavia and Clark (Education Sciences, 2025) gathered stakeholder perspectives on the real barriers facing twice-exceptional students in Australian schools, and organise what they found into five themes. First, a policy gap: disability support is mandatory while gifted education is discretionary, creating what the authors call “policy asymmetry” that leaves twice-exceptional students falling between two systems. Second, teachers are given little to no training — in initial teacher education or ongoing professional development — in how to identify or support this population. Third, the paper offers a genuinely useful four-point explanation of why identification fails: masking effects, behaviour-first interpretations (reading a student through their behaviour rather than their profile), output-dependent bias (judging ability by what a student produces rather than what they understand), and executive function being misread as something else entirely. Fourth, mainstream classrooms typically lack the differentiated, strengths-based instruction these students need. Fifth, without school-based support, families absorb the advocacy, financial and emotional cost themselves, sometimes to the point of homeschooling. The paper’s central idea is that these failures don’t just add up — they multiply each other, producing a compounded disadvantage that is worse than any single gap on its own.

  • What is twice exceptionality? — AAEGT
    Read summary

    The Australian Association for the Education of the Gifted and Talented’s explainer introduces twice-exceptionality for a general audience of parents and educators, using the working description that young children can show early signs of giftedness together with a learning difficulty at the same time — the two are not mutually exclusive, and one does not rule out the other. The page situates twice-exceptionality within broader gifted identification processes, noting how easily one exceptionality can obscure the other when schools are only looking for a single profile: a student’s giftedness can mask a genuine learning difficulty, keeping them from getting support, while a diagnosed learning difficulty can just as easily mask real giftedness, keeping a capable student from being challenged. It’s included here as an accessible, Australian-context entry point for teachers who are new to the concept, sitting alongside the more research-heavy citations on this page as a plain-language starting reference rather than a primary study.
